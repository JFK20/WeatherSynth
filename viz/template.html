Stochastic solar model — the fitted clear-sky index

Daily irradiance is never modelled directly. It is split into a deterministic ceiling (solar geometry and atmosphere) and a bounded random fraction of it, the clear-sky index. This page shows the distribution fitted to the measured record, and what a synthetic record drawn from it does and does not reproduce.

The distribution being modelled

The measured histogram against the fitted Beta. Beta is used because it is bounded at both ends, so a draw can never be negative and never runs away — the generator needs no clamp. The support runs to 1.25, not 1.0: the ceiling carries a monthly-mean turbidity, so a day cleaner than its month's average genuinely beats it.

Seasonal profile

One Beta per calendar month, so the model carries the season. Sampling the fitted model reproduces the measured monthly means closely — which it should, since matching the first two moments is exactly what the fit does. This panel confirms the wiring, not the physics.

A year of daily irradiation

The same year, measured and generated, each under its clear-sky ceiling. The totals agree and the spread agrees. Look at the texture instead: the measured year moves in runs — dull weeks and bright weeks — while the synthetic one flickers between them, because every day is drawn independently of the one before it.

What this model still gets wrong

Each day plotted against the day before it. A cloud of points along the diagonal means weather persists; a shapeless blob means each day forgets the last. The measured record persists and the model does not — this is the single clearest statement of what is missing, and the reason a Markov chain is the next step rather than a refinement.
