Stochastic weather model — the fitted clear-sky index

Daily irradiance is never modelled directly. It is split into a deterministic ceiling (solar geometry and atmosphere) and a bounded random fraction of it, the clear-sky index. This page shows the distribution fitted to the measured record, and what a synthetic record drawn from it does and does not reproduce.

The distribution being modelled

The measured histogram against the fitted Beta. Beta is used because it is bounded at both ends, so a draw can never be negative and never runs away — the generator needs no clamp. The support runs to 1.25, not 1.0: the ceiling carries a monthly-mean turbidity, so a day cleaner than its month's average genuinely beats it.

Does the model remember yesterday?

Each day plotted against the day before it. A cloud of points along the diagonal means weather persists; a shapeless blob means each day forgets the last. The synthetic panel used to be that blob — the histograms matched and the sequences did not. An AR(1) term on a hidden variable now carries the memory across, reordering days without touching the fitted monthly shapes, and the diagonal appears.

The distribution being modelled

The measured histogram against two fitted Weibulls. Wind has no ceiling to divide out, so unlike the solar half the modelled quantity is the speed itself — m/s, at one height, over one averaging window. The textbook two-parameter Weibull must put density all the way down to zero; freeing a location parameter γ lets the curve start where this site's daily means actually start, around 1 m/s.

Seasonal profile

One Weibull per calendar month. The whole seasonal cycle lives in these twelve fits — there is no deterministic ceiling underneath carrying it, as there is for solar. Replacing them with a single pooled fit would not lose a refinement, it would lose the seasons.

Does the model remember yesterday?

The same latent AR(1) and Gaussian copula the solar half uses — the chain never learns whether the distribution behind it is a Beta or a Weibull. Judge it in latent space, not here: a copula reproduces the hidden correlation exactly, while the correlation of the speeds themselves depends on the marginal's shape, so wind lands a few percent short of its measured figure where solar matches to four digits.

The cube law, and what the model does not reproduce

Wind power goes as v³, and the mean of cubes always exceeds the cube of the mean — so cubing a daily mean speed understates that day's energy. The measured correction varies day to day, drawn here. The model applies the record's average instead of drawing one, the single value marked below, which fixes the systematic bias and flattens the spread. Right for an annual yield; too smooth for anything that cares about the distribution of daily energy.
